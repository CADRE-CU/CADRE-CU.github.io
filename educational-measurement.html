Educational Measurement

The technical and conceptual foundations of testing — psychometric modeling, scaling and growth, validity and fairness — and the question that sits behind all of it: how do we know what students have learned?

Overview

A test score is a number standing in for a claim about what a student knows and can do. Educational measurement is the study of when, and how well, that claim holds up.

CADRE's measurement work spans the full arc from theory to practice: the models that turn item responses into scores, the scales that let scores be compared across grades and years, and the validity arguments that connect scores to the interpretations placed on them. Much of this work is done in partnership with assessment developers and state agencies, so that methodological questions get posed in the settings where their answers matter.

We also attend to the history and philosophy of measurement in the human sciences. Understanding where psychometric conventions came from, and what they take for granted, is part of deciding which of them deserve to be carried forward.

Questions guiding our work

  • When scores from different grades or test forms are placed on a common scale, what does a difference of ten points actually mean?
  • How can a learning progression anchor a scale so that growth is described in terms of what students have learned to do?
  • Which items function differently for different groups of students, and what should be done about it?
  • What must be true of a test score before it can support a causal or policy inference?
Research strands

Where we focus inside educational measurement.

Four overlapping areas of inquiry that anchor current and recent work.

θ

Psychometric Modeling

Item response theory, Rasch measurement, explanatory item response models, and diagnostic classification models — and what each assumes about the units it produces.

↗

Vertical Scales & Growth

How scales that span grades are built, how sensitive growth interpretations are to those design decisions, and how content-referenced reporting can make growth interpretable.

⚖

Validity & Fairness

Validity arguments for high-stakes uses, differential item functioning, and the group differences that item formats and test designs can introduce.

§

Foundations of Measurement

The history and philosophy of measurement in the human sciences, from the concept of a unit to the question of whether psychological attributes are quantitative at all.

Lead faculty

Who's leading this work at CADRE.

Measurement is the historical core of CADRE's work and of the Research and Evaluation Methodology program that houses it.

DB

Derek C. Briggs

Director, CADRE · Professor, Research & Evaluation Methodology

Briggs's research examines how measurement works, and how it can mislead, in educational assessment: vertical scaling and growth, item response theory and the Rasch model, learning progressions as a basis for scale construction, and the history and philosophy of measurement in the human sciences. He is a past president of the National Council on Measurement in Education, a Fellow of AERA, and a member of the National Academy of Education.

Profile Personal site
BS

Benjamin R. Shear

Associate Professor, Research & Evaluation Methodology · CADRE Faculty Partner

Shear studies the development, interpretation, and use of large-scale assessments. His work includes differential item functioning and item-format effects, diagnostic classification models, methods for recovering test score distributions from coarsened data that underlie the Stanford Education Data Archive, and the measurement issues that arise when test scores are used for causal inference.

Methods & approach

How we answer these questions.

Our work combines psychometric theory with the applied statistics needed to put it to the test on real assessment data.

Item response & Rasch modeling Unidimensional and multidimensional IRT, explanatory item response models, diagnostic classification models
Scaling & linking Vertical scale construction, equating, common-item designs, and sensitivity analyses
Validity argumentation Interpretation and use arguments, DIF analysis, and evidence for high-stakes inferences
Simulation & applied statistics Monte Carlo studies, latent variable models, and methods for coarsened data
