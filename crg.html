Content-Referenced Growth

A new way to report what students have learned. Instead of telling a teacher that a score went up 20 points, content-referenced growth describes the change in terms of the skills and understandings a student has gained.

Try the fractions prototype Read the 2025 paper
Overview

A 20-point gain tells a teacher that a student grew. It doesn't say in what.

Most growth reports are norm-referenced or criterion-referenced: they tell you where a student stands relative to peers, or whether a target was met. Content-referenced growth (CRG) reporting takes a different approach. It anchors a test's score scale to a learning progression, so that a change in score can be read as a change in how a student thinks about a topic, illustrated in concrete terms by the kinds of items the student can now answer.

CADRE has been developing CRG reporting since 2020 in a research-practice partnership with Curriculum Associates, using the i-Ready Diagnostic. The work has produced learning progressions in mathematics and reading, an interactive reporting prototype, a series of teacher studies, and a 2025 article in Educational Assessment.

What changes for teachers and families

The same growth, described two ways.

Before
“Your child grew 60 points in math.”

A norm- or target-referenced report. It says that growth happened, and perhaps whether it was enough, but not what the student can do now that they couldn't before.

After
“Your child has progressed from a ‘part-whole’ view of fractions to understanding them on a number line.”

A content-referenced report. The same score change, read against a learning progression, becomes a statement about how the student's thinking has developed and what comes next.

How it works

Four building blocks.

CRG reporting rests on four elements that have to be designed together.

1

Learning Progression

A research-based description of the successively more sophisticated ways students think about a topic, organized into levels that mark significant steps in understanding.

2

Targeted Test Design

Tests whose item content overlaps across occasions and grades, so that a common scale can be established and each progression level is represented by enough items.

3

Vertical Scale (Rasch Model)

Occasion-specific tests are placed on a single vertical scale. The Rasch model's invariance property keeps distances along the scale interpretable regardless of which items a student saw.

4

Item Mapping

Exemplar items are located on the scale and linked to progression levels, so that a numerical distance can be read as an instructionally meaningful difference in understanding.

Theory of action

From a reporting interface to student learning.

The building blocks matter only if they change what teachers infer and do. This is the chain of reasoning the project is designed to test.

Reporting interface

Built on the learning progression, test design, Rasch scale, and item map.

Educator interaction

Teachers explore a student's status and growth against progression levels and exemplar items.

Inferences about learning

Score changes are interpreted as changes in thinking, not as points gained.

Changes in instruction

Teachers make conceptual, not just procedural, adjustments and group students by what they need next.

Student learning outcomes

The end goal, with teacher professional learning and attitudes about assessment as secondary outcomes.

Professional development supports the interaction and inference steps. The interface is meant as a starting point for inquiry about a student's thinking, not a diagnosis.

CRG in action

A learning progression for fractions.

The first prototype is built around four increasingly sophisticated ways of understanding a fraction. Each level is anchored to i-Ready Diagnostic items whose difficulty rises with the level.

  1. 1

    Part-Whole

    A number of parts out of a total

    A fraction is some number of equal parts out of a whole, often understood through visual models such as a partitioned shape.

  2. 2

    Quotient

    “Fair shares”

    A fraction represents the division of a whole into equal shares, so three-fourths is three things shared fairly among four.

  3. 3

    Measurement

    “Number line”

    Fractions are magnitudes that can be located on a number line. This is where equivalent fractions become meaningful, a prerequisite for adding fractions with unlike denominators.

  4. 4

    Operator

    “Multiply and divide”

    A fraction transforms an original value into a new, proportionally related value through multiplication and division.

Questions

Frequently asked.

How is CRG different from norm-referenced and criterion-referenced reporting?

Norm-referenced reports locate a student relative to peers, for example at the 60th percentile. Criterion-referenced reports compare a score to a target, for example “met the annual growth goal.” Both are useful for monitoring, and neither says much about what the student has learned.

CRG reporting uses qualitative distinctions among assessment items to interpret numerical distances along a score scale. Because the items are mapped to the levels of a learning progression, a change in score can be described as movement from one way of thinking about a topic to a more sophisticated one. The intent is formative: to give teachers a starting point for conceptual, rather than only procedural, adjustments to instruction.

How was the fractions learning progression validated?

Two kinds of evidence support the progression's structure. First, the order in which i-Ready Classroom Mathematics lessons introduce fractions skills correlates with the progression level of those skills (rank correlation of 0.68). Second, the difficulty of i-Ready Diagnostic items correlates with their progression level (rank correlation of 0.60), and the difference in difficulty across levels holds after controlling for grade. Item difficulty distributions overlap at adjacent levels, but the overall trend supports the ordering.

The underlying working paper proposes five interpretations of a fraction, adding ratio/rate between measurement and operator. The reporting prototype presents four levels.

What did teachers do with the prototype?

Seven teachers experienced with i-Ready worked through the fractions prototype in think-aloud sessions. They connected scale scores to progression levels and to the problem types students at each level could handle. Some began with their existing, target-based interpretation of i-Ready growth and then moved to content-referenced interpretations; all understood the hierarchy of levels, though some questioned the grain size of particular levels.

Three uses came up repeatedly:

  • Instructional grouping. Six of seven teachers saw the prototype as a way to form groups for targeted work on fractions.
  • Communicating with families and students. Three teachers described using it in conferences to explain what a score means in terms of specific learning milestones.
  • Professional learning. Five teachers saw it as a way to deepen their own content knowledge, especially for newer teachers or those less familiar with mathematics standards.
What would make CRG interpretations more trustworthy?
  • Professional development. Teachers need support to move from a “count up points” mindset to a focus on qualitative changes in reasoning, so that CRG is used for its intended formative purposes.
  • Careful exemplar items. The items chosen to represent a level shape how teachers read it. Selecting items that represent the most cognitively demanding application of a level's conceptualization, or showing three to four items per level, would better convey the variability within a level.
  • Better item formats. The i-Ready Diagnostic relies mostly on selected-response items. Open-ended tasks, ordered multiple-choice formats, or AI-supported interactive tasks would reveal student thinking more directly.
  • Triangulation. CRG results should serve as a starting point for inquiry, not a diagnosis. Teachers should check them against evidence from classroom tasks that are not subject to the constraints of standardized items.
What's next for the project?
  • Real data trials. Observing teachers using the interface with their actual students rather than hypothetical ones.
  • Impact on inferences and actions. Investigating empirically whether the interface leads teachers to the intended instructional inferences and subsequent actions.
  • Outcomes. Evaluating whether CRG influences teacher attitudes toward assessment, instructional practice, and ultimately student learning.
  • Scale invariance. Testing whether vertical scales maintain the generalizability and interpretability of reference distances across grades and forms.

Learning is messy. CRG is designed to generate substantive questions for teachers rather than to hand them answers, and to foster deeper engagement with how students' understanding develops.

Where does the idea come from?

Two main inspirations: the item maps published with the National Assessment of Educational Progress, and two decades of research on learning progressions and trajectories.
